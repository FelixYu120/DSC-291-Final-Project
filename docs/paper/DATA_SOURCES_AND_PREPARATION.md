# Data sources and preparation for Zimbabwe maize yield estimation

## Study data and analytical scope

We assembled crop-cut yield observations, satellite reflectance and gridded meteorological data to study retrospective maize-yield estimation in Zimbabwe. The observational unit was a provider-recorded field-season. Our primary comparison evaluated whether observations from a second provider and harvest year improved estimation for the original Lacuna 2023 population. Because the sources lack a verified common farm identifier, we concatenated harmonized field-season records and preserved provider and harvest-year labels; spatial proximity was not treated as evidence that observations represented the same farm. All sample counts below are derived from the retained source files and processing audits.

The first source was the PULA Advisors Lacuna release, version 1.1.1, published in November 2023 [1]. Its Zimbabwe GeoJSON contains 2,269 maize observations, including harvests in 2022 and 2023. The provider describes a randomly located 8 × 5 m crop cut, with weights recorded at harvest and after drying. Planting and harvest dates, management indicators and point coordinates accompany the yield observations. The second source was the Zimbabwe subset of the Scalable Vision-Guided Crop Yield Estimation Dataset, comprising 5,055 maize records from 2024 and released in November 2025 [2]. Its associated study describes yield as the average of two randomly located crop cuts [3]. We used the released yield metadata and coordinates, rather than the photographs, as inputs to the satellite-based analysis. Our screened population differs from the associated study's 4,173-field Zimbabwe sample, which additionally excludes zones with fewer than 20 observations.

## Measurement validation and harmonization

We reconciled provider identifiers, coordinates and yield values against the frozen project labels before constructing modeling tables. The Lacuna audit required finite yields between 0 and 30 t ha⁻¹, finite nonnegative wet and dry masses, dry mass no greater than wet mass, and coordinates within a coarse Zimbabwe envelope of 25.1–33.1° E and 22.5–15.5° S. The original primary-model policy imposed a further 15 t ha⁻¹ upper limit and restricted observations to rainfed, sole-cropped maize harvested in 2023. The 2024 audit used a 0–15 t ha⁻¹ yield screen, the same coordinate envelope, at least one measured crop cut, and consistency between available crop-cut measurements and the reported field average. These bounds are validation rules, not independent assessments of field location or biological plausibility.

For a crop cut of area A square metres and reported dry mass w kilograms, yield in tonnes per hectare is 10w/A; a 40 m² cut therefore implies 0.25w t ha⁻¹. The Lacuna manual identifies the units as Mg ha⁻¹, consistent with metric tonnes and the mass–area arithmetic, despite contradictory wording on the dataset landing page. We retained the original provisional-unit flag and did not interpret the numerical agreement as evidence of a standardized moisture content. Lacuna conversion discrepancies were assessed with tolerance equal to the larger of 0.001 t ha⁻¹ and 0.1% of the larger absolute yield value. Three records implied a factor of 0.5 rather than 0.25 and were excluded because the released data did not identify whether mass or sampled area was incorrect. This left 2,266 Lacuna records: 169 from 2022 and 2,097 from 2023.

For 2024, reported per-cut yields were numerically consistent with a 40 m² normalization, or 0.25 times dry mass in kilograms. This is an arithmetic check: neither the released CSV nor the associated paper's methods section independently specifies the physical cut area. We retained the reported field yields after checking their agreement with the mean of available cuts, using absolute tolerance 10⁻⁶ t ha⁻¹ for box conversions and averages. An already averaged yield was not divided by the number of cuts again. We excluded 723 records without yield measurements, retaining 4,332 records, including 42 with one measured cut and 1,874 recorded zero yields. Recorded zeros were preserved as observations rather than recoded as missing; the original primary Lacuna population likewise retained 28 zeros. The resulting catalog contained 6,598 screened field-season records (Table 1).

Neither source provides the moisture percentages needed for a physical moisture-basis correction. Uncertainty also remains in drying completeness, grain or cob basis, and within-field sampling error. We therefore preserved provider-specific measurement metadata, cut counts and uncertainty flags. Statistical source adjustment in the prediction models addresses differences in observed distributions; it does not establish physical equivalence of the two measurement protocols. Repeated coordinates were retained and assigned to spatial groups rather than assumed to be duplicate field observations.

## Satellite observations and temporal features

We used the existing project snapshot of Sentinel-2 Collection 1 Level-2A bottom-of-atmosphere reflectance, accessed through the Earth Search STAC collection `sentinel-2-c1-l2a` [4,5]. The ten bands were B02, B03, B04, B05, B06, B07, B08, B8A, B11 and B12. Reflectance was placed on a 10 m output grid using bilinear resampling, while the categorical scene-classification layer used nearest-neighbour resampling. STAC radiometric scale and offset were applied once. Resampling the native 20 m bands does not create measurements with native 10 m spatial resolution.

For each field-month, candidate scenes with tile cloud cover at most 80% were ordered by cloud cover, acquisition time and item identifier. The downloader attempted up to three candidates and retained the first satisfying its validity criterion. Pixels required finite values in all selected bands and scene-classification codes 4, 5 or 6, corresponding to vegetation, nonvegetated land and water. The minimum valid fraction within the extraction support was 0.8. All Zimbabwe records have point geometry, and every accepted Zimbabwe satellite observation in the pinned database contains one valid grid cell at the recorded point. Consequently, the extraction routine's spatial median reduces to that single value. These measurements are neither whole-field averages nor monthly composites of all available acquisitions. The separately developed stricter cloud-buffer variant was not substituted into the reported experiments.

Five spectral features were computed from each retained monthly reflectance vector before temporal aggregation. Their exact implemented definitions are NDVI = (B08 − B04)/(B08 + B04), GCVI = B08/B03 − 1, NDVI705 = (B08 − B05)/(B08 + B05), NDVI740 = (B08 − B06)/(B08 + B06), and an MTCI-labelled ratio = (B08 − B05)/(B05 − B04). The last feature is an implementation-specific near-infrared/red-edge ratio that uses B08 in place of B06 in the Sentinel-2 MTCI definition [6]. Its archived name was retained for traceability. Undefined or nonfinite ratios were marked missing. Predictor names were preserved to maintain consistency with the frozen feature tables and model results.

We aligned the providers on December–June relative to harvest year: December 2022–June 2023 for the target population and December 2023–June 2024 for the auxiliary source. October and November 2022 were unavailable in the pinned Lacuna collection. Eligibility for the aggregation comparison required at least four observed months in the common full-season window and finite predictors in every compared aggregation. All 169 Lacuna 2022 records failed this rule: 101 had one observed month and 68 had two. Two 2024 records also failed. This establishes insufficient coverage in the retained collection, rather than absence of imagery from every possible archive.

The baseline aggregation used full-season means (V0). Alternatives were a crop-date preharvest mean (V1), the componentwise seasonal maximum (V2), and separate seasonal median and half-sample modal estimates (V3); the V3 mean is identical to V0. V1 retained observations from the later of December 1 and recorded planting, up to but excluding recorded harvest, within December–June. Because 2024 crop dates are unavailable, its V1 window was December–March. An additional control applied December–March to both providers. The typical maize calendar supports this proxy but does not supply field-specific dates [7]. Shortened windows required observed values, without imposing a new four-month threshold that would change the comparison population. The modal estimator follows a shortest-half recursion, averaging estimates across tied intervals; this tie treatment is an explicitly specified adaptation of the half-sample approach [8].

Missing months were omitted when forming the aggregate features, and the latest aggregation models rejected any missing predictor rather than imputing imagery. Earlier models that retained the full monthly sequence used training-fold median imputation and explicit missing-scene indicators. The original compact baseline contained ten index mean-and-maximum features. The richer monthly representation contained 122 predictors: those ten summaries, 105 band/index-by-month measurements and seven missing-scene indicators. Reflectance-band and index aggregation families were evaluated separately.

A separate representation diagnostic used 128-dimensional Presto embeddings [9] already present in the pinned team database. We loaded these frozen vectors without regenerating them or retraining the encoder. Their whole-window coverage and masking conventions differ from those of the common-window spectral features. Their comparison therefore assesses the supplied representations rather than isolating the encoder while holding every input convention fixed. Retained feature matrices support replay of the downstream models.

## Meteorological enrichment

We obtained monthly corrected precipitation rate (`PRECTOTCORR`, mm day⁻¹) and 2 m air temperature (`T2M`, °C) for 1991–2024 from the NASA Langley Research Center POWER Monthly and Annual API [10]. The archived responses were retrieved on 7 November 2026 UTC and identify API version 2.10.0, MERRA2 meteorology and local solar time. Each WGS84 point was assigned to the nearest native 0.5° latitude × 0.625° longitude grid centre, with exact half-cell ties assigned toward the increasing grid index. The full catalog occupied 97 cells. These regional reanalysis estimates do not measure field-level weather; the median point-to-centre distance was 22.98 km and the maximum was 42.92 km.

Monthly precipitation depth was calculated by multiplying the reported mean daily rate by the actual number of days in the month, including leap days. Temperature remained a monthly mean. Cell-specific calendar-month normals were calculated from 1991–2020, independently of yield observations. Rainfall z-scores used the historical mean and sample standard deviation of monthly rainfall totals; temperature anomalies were differences from the corresponding calendar-month mean. Seasonal rainfall percent of normal used the ratio of observed rainfall totals to normal daily rainfall rates accumulated over the actual month lengths. Temperature summaries and anomalies were weighted by month length. The weather season ran from October of the preceding year through June of harvest year.

The full weather representation comprised 46 variables: nine monthly values for each of rainfall amount, temperature, rainfall z-score and temperature anomaly, plus ten seasonal summaries. These summaries comprised rainfall total and percentage of normal for October–June, October–March and January–March, and mean temperature and temperature anomaly for October–June and January–March. The compact alternatives used January–March rainfall percent of normal and temperature anomaly, with a four-variable version adding the corresponding October–December summaries. These were derived from the same archived responses. Monthly means were not used to infer daily dry spells, heat-wave duration or field-specific crop-stage stress.

## Spatial partitions and preprocessing

Labels and field-level features were joined using unique source-prefixed observation identifiers; monthly observations used the pair of observation identifier and calendar month. Weather joins were many-to-one through grid cell and month or harvest season. Join validation enforced key uniqueness, row counts and preservation of the recorded outcomes. Provider, year, management, measurement and eligibility metadata remained available for auditing rather than entering the ordinary predictor matrix, except for an explicit source-domain indicator in merged tree models.

The original primary population comprised 1,880 rainfed, sole-cropped Lacuna 2023 observations, obtained by excluding 212 intercropped and five irrigated records from the screened 2023 catalog. Spatial groups were defined by 2 km single-linkage connected components and assigned to five size-balanced folds. Current comparisons excluded the 376 observations in historical fold 0 from fitting and scoring, and used the remaining 1,504 observations in four outer folds of 376. Each outer fit therefore used 1,128 original target-training observations, with three inner group-based folds for model selection. Spatial blocking addresses dependence in evaluation [11]; the inherited 2 km threshold was not an empirically estimated decorrelation distance.

Auxiliary 2024 observations within 2 km of reserved or outer-evaluation points were excluded from each outer fit; inner fits additionally protected inner-validation points. The expanded-training experiment applied the same procedure to the 217 additional Lacuna 2023 candidates, permitting 132, 149, 138 and 150 additional observations in the four outer fits. All variants retained the same original 1,504 evaluation fields. The expansion therefore tests broader management coverage and auxiliary-source use, rather than a contribution from 2022 imagery or prediction across all three harvest years.

Within each fit, imagery imputation where applicable, 1st/99th percentile clipping and feature standardization were estimated only from permitted Lacuna training observations. Weather standardization used training observations without percentile clipping, preserving extreme weather values. Adaptive Ridge used shared and provider-specific feature terms motivated by feature augmentation [12], with training-cohort centering, outcome scaling and source-weight selection. Separate shared-coefficient Ridge models pooled the original yield values with tuned source weights. Evaluation outcomes did not determine these transformations or tuning choices. Full model specifications and grids accompany the released experiments.

## Reproducibility and scope of inference

Versioned releases preserve raw-source metadata, screened labels, exclusion reasons, feature dictionaries, exact split memberships, spatial permissions, selected parameters and field-level held-out predictions. SHA-256 manifests identify the source files, database, configuration, implementation and derived outputs. Provider identifiers and source-row references support record tracing; raw-provider file hashes are recorded separately from the legacy label-column hash of the screened audit table. The original shared database and earlier releases were preserved. The analysis snapshot is identified by repository revision `a8f31fb`, with immutable output and input fingerprints in each release [13]. Both yield datasets retain their CC BY 4.0 attribution. Meteorological attribution is to NASA Langley Research Center POWER, funded through NASA's Earth Science Division.

Predictions were constrained to be nonnegative before scoring. RMSE and predictive R² were calculated from the pooled held-out predictions on the original t ha⁻¹ scale, with one prediction per evaluation field; R² was not averaged over folds. Paired 95% percentile intervals used 2,000 spatial-group resamples, conditional on saved predictions rather than model refitting, with weather-cell sensitivity analyses where appropriate. Repeatedly used development folds and historical controls do not constitute independent replications or an untouched blind test. Moreover, 1,447 of the 1,504 evaluation fields shared a weather grid cell with target-training fields in their respective outer fits. The results therefore concern the stated retrospective target population and do not demonstrate transfer to entirely new weather regions.

Full-season imagery and weather can extend beyond harvest, actual crop dates are retrospective metadata, and the auxiliary training source postdates the target harvest. Accordingly, these experiments estimate yield retrospectively rather than establish operational 2023 forecasting. Remaining constraints include unknown moisture basis, point-to-field mismatch, permissive scene classification, sparse seasonal observations, coarse weather resolution and confounding of provider with harvest year. These limitations determine the interpretation of any gain from harmonization or additional predictors.
